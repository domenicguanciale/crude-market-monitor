Crude Market Monitor in 3D

Five views of the same research, on one time axis from 1986 to today. It opens at the start of the 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption, with the whole price history behind it. The price terrain turns Brent and WTI into walls you can fly along, with a pin on every spike the rules found. The skyline compares every week since 1986 by volatility, price or the US tightness score. The globe shows tanker traffic through six shipping chokepoints, the Hormuz close-up puts one ship on the map for each tanker transit per day, and world flows shows measured crude production and US imports by country. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, and press play or drag the slider to move through time.

How to read this

  • Shows what moved together, not what caused what. Tanker counts, prices and the score come from different sources and do not prove one another.
  • Price terrain. Two walls, Brent at the back and WTI in front. The top edge is the daily spot price and the colour is the 20-day volatility. WTI's wall drops below the floor on April 20, 2020, the day it settled below zero. Pins mark spikes found by fixed rules. A pin explains a spike only after it has been hand-checked against its sources.
  • Volatility. Realized volatility is the standard deviation of daily price changes over 20 trading days, scaled to a year. The readout ranks it against every trading day since 1987.
  • World flows. Measured data only: crude production by country (EIA international data) and US crude imports by country of origin (EIA). Worldwide trade between other countries is not published in a source this page may republish, so it is not drawn.
  • Tanker pillars. Height is the 7-day average of tankers per day. The ring on each pillar marks that chokepoint's own median for 2019 to 2025, so a pillar below its ring is running under its usual traffic. Counts come from satellite ship signals and miss ships that switch them off.
  • Skyline. One bar per week. Columns run Jan to Dec, rows run 1986 (back) to 2026 (front). In the tightness view, bars above the floor mean the US market is tight against the same week in the prior five years, bars below it mean loose. Bars after the selected date are faded.
  • The 2020 switch. The default leaves 2020 out of every five-year range, because the pandemic swelled stocks and widens the range. Keep it in and 2021 to 2025 read tighter. Same data, different method.
  • US data only. The score ignores the rest of the world, which is why Brent minus WTI sits beside it. A wide gap with a "normal" score is a global shortage the US data does not show.

Tanker counts (IMF PortWatch) start in 2019, the tightness score in November 1995 and Brent in May 1987; before those dates the views say no data. Prices on weekends and holidays carry the last close forward. The weekly score is dated to the Friday that ends the week, and EIA publishes it the following Wednesday.
